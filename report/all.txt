Executive summary
This report stress-tests risk models built on the COMPAS two-year recidivism file (n = 7,214; seed 450;
5,049 training and 2,165 test rows). A linear model of the COMPAS decile score (Model A) and a logistic
model of actual two-year recidivism (Model B; test accuracy 67.9%, AUC 0.734) disagree on 296 test
defendants (13.67%), almost all of them flagged by Model A only. Four stress tests follow. (1) No genuine
sign reversal exists; the clearest subgroup difference is a stronger prior-record effect for women (1.024
versus 0.666 for men), which shrinks once priors are log-transformed. (2) Omitting priors_count turns the
race coefficient from 0.098 (not significant) into 0.302 (p < 0.001). (3) 90 confident errors (4.16% of the
test set) are almost exactly as many as the model's own probabilities predict. (4) Across thresholds 0.05 to
0.95, demographic parity and equalized odds rise and fall together (r = 0.998); the real cost of parity is
accuracy and predictive parity. I recommend conditional, advisory-only use at t = 0.45 (DP 0.507, EO
0.478, PP 0.920, accuracy 66.8%), with the limitations documented below.

1 Introduction and objectives
Predictive models used in high-stakes decisions can look statistically sound while concealing bias, hidden
confounding and fairness trade-offs. This assignment asks for a model to be built, deliberately attacked,
and then defended in writing using only numbers the student computed. The objectives are: (i) fit a linear
(Model A) and a logistic (Model B) model on the assigned dataset and compare the high-risk labels they
produce; (ii) run four stress tests: a Simpson's-paradox hunt, an omitted-variable experiment, an
adversarial subset of confident errors, and a fairness-threshold sweep; (iii) answer eight analytical
questions; and (iv) reflect on whether a policy-maker can responsibly be given “just one number”. My roll
number (0450) ends in 0, so the assigned dataset is the COMPAS Recidivism Risk Score file, and the
random seed and split are 450 and 70/30. All results come from one notebook that regenerates every
table, figure and number in this document.

2 Dataset description
The data are ProPublica’s compas-scores-two-years.csv (7,214 defendants, 53 columns) from Broward
County, Florida, downloaded from the URL in the assignment (SHA-256 recorded in Appendix C).
Defendant IDs are unique; decile_score ranges from 1 to 10. Target-variable check. The brief names a
binary outcome is_recidivist, but no such column exists. Two candidates do: two_year_recid and is_recid,
which disagree on 220 people who reoffended after the two-year window. I therefore define is_recidivist :=
two_year_recid because it alone has a fixed two-year follow-up; its base rate is 45.1% (versus 48.1% for
is_recid). A sensitivity check with is_recid (Appendix B) changes AUC by only 0.005. Features are age,
priors_count, three juvenile-record counts, sex, charge degree (felony/misdemeanour) and race. The
recidivism rate differs by group (Table 1), as do prior records and age, which matters for every fairness
result that follows.

3 Data preprocessing methodology
Each preprocessing decision is documented below, as the assignment requires. No outcome-derived or
COMPAS-output column is used as a predictor, and every fitted transformation (the scaler) is estimated on
the training set only.

Missing values

22 of the 53 columns contain missing values (dates, charge descriptions, follow-up and
violent-recidivism fields). None of them is used as a predictor, so no imputation is needed and no
row is dropped (n = 7,214); the ten model features have zero missing values (asserted in the

Leakage control

Excluded as predictors: two_year_recid, is_recid, every r_*, vr_* and violent_recid column

Feature scaling

The five numeric features (age, priors_count, juv_fel_count, juv_misd_count, juv_other_count) are

5 Part 2.1 – Simpson’s-paradox hunt
Design. Inference models were fitted on all 7,214 rows (full) and on each subgroup with identical features
(the split variable omitted). Differences are tested with z = (βA − βB)/√(seA² + seB²). Because I was
searching for a reversal, I first scanned three demographic splits (race, sex, age ≤ 30 vs > 30) over every
predictor: 26 comparisons, so the Bonferroni threshold is p < 0.0019. Finding 1: no genuine reversal. 5
of the 26 coefficients change sign between subgroups, but in none of them are both subgroup coefficients
significantly non-zero (0 of 5), and none is significant after correction; they are sampling noise around
zero. For example, the race coefficient is 0.111 [−0.017, 0.238] for men and −0.231 [−0.491, 0.029] for
women (difference p = 0.021, Bonferroni p = 0.54); I report it as an exploratory observation, not a finding.
Finding 2: a significantly stronger effect. The priors_count effect is 1.024 [0.813, 1.235] for women
(B, n = 1,395) versus 0.666 [0.594, 0.738] for men (A, n = 5,819); full data 0.721. The intervals do not
overlap (z = 3.15, p = 0.0016; Bonferroni p = 0.043). It is the only sex-split difference that survives
correction (age-split differences are partly mechanical because age is both the splitter and a predictor).
Diagnostic. Entering priors as log(1 + priors) shrinks the gap to 0.666 (men) versus 0.811 (women), p =
0.075, so much of the heterogeneity reflects functional form: women have fewer priors (mean 2.28 versus
3.76). The race split, which matters most for Section 2.4, yields priors_count coefficients of 0.664
(African-American) versus 0.856 (Caucasian), nominal p = 0.021 but Bonferroni p = 0.55: suggestive, not
established (Table 9).

6 Part 2.2 – Omitted-variable bias
Choice of confounder: priors_count. It is the strongest predictor of recidivism (full-model β = 0.701,
training-set correlation with the outcome r = 0.27) and is correlated with the other predictors, including
race_African_American (r = 0.19), juv_misd_count (r = 0.22) and age (r = 0.17), so removing it should
push its explanatory power onto them. It is also shaped by policing intensity, which is why it is a
contestable variable. The full model is Model B (training set); the reduced model drops only priors_count.
Formula: %Δ = (βreduced − βfull) / |βfull| × 100; because this explodes when βfull ≈ 0, Table 10 also reports
the absolute change and the change in units of the full model’s standard error. Results. The largest
percentage change is juv_misd_count (−0.018 → 0.109, +708%, a sign flip), but its baseline is almost zero,
so this is a numerical artefact. The largest absolute and standardised shift is age (−0.526 → −0.312,
+40.6%, 5.9 standard errors). The most consequential is race_African_American: 0.098 (p = 0.158) →
0.302 (p < 0.001), +207.7%. 4 coefficients (the three juvenile counts and race_African_American) turn
from non-significant to significant and none loses significance. Test AUC falls from 0.734 to 0.664.
Caveats. Logistic coefficients are non-collapsible (they change when covariates are dropped even without
confounding), and priors may be a mediator rather than a confounder; the table shows how fragile
coefficient interpretations are, not the causal effect of any variable.

7 Part 2.3 – Adversarial subset construction
Extraction. On the 2,165 test rows there are 35 false positives (P ≥ 0.75, actual 0) and 55 false negatives
(P ≤ 0.25, actual 1): 90 confident errors, 4.16% of the test set. Because more than 50 qualify, 50
were drawn at random (random_state = 450): 21 false positives and 29 false negatives. Profile.
Compared with the full test set, the subset contains no one under 25 (0% versus 20.0%), is much older
(median age 47.5 versus 31; 56% aged 45+ versus 21.2%), and has a different gender mix (72% male
versus 80.0%). It is two mirror-image clusters rather than one: all 21 false positives have ≥ 5 priors
(median 18; 81% African-American, 95% male), whereas the 29 false negatives have none with ≥ 5 priors
(76% have at most one, 83% are 45 or older, 17% African-American). Why the model fails confidently.
(a) Calibrated uncertainty: among the 180 test defendants with P ≥ 0.75 the observed recidivism rate is
80.6% (mean predicted 84.5%), and among the 331 with P ≤ 0.25 it is 16.6% (predicted 18.2%). A
calibrated model should therefore make about 28.0 confident false positives and 60.4 confident false
negatives (88.4 in total); the observed 35 and 55 (90) are close, so most of these errors are irreducible
outcome noise. (b) Ignored non-linearity: adding squared and interaction terms improves fit significantly
(LR χ² = 81.8, df = 6, p < 0.001) with priors² = −0.086 (diminishing returns) and age² = 0.196 (older
defendants are not as safe as a straight line implies), but test AUC moves only from 0.734 to 0.739 and
the count of confident errors does not fall (90 → 114). Misspecification contributes, yet it does not explain
most of the failures.

Question 1
Why did the threshold-based approach and the direct logistic approach flag, or not flag, the same individuals as high
risk? Quote the mismatch count and percentage, and compare the median linear prediction with the 0.5 threshold.

The two rules disagreed on 296 of 2,165 test defendants (13.67%) and agreed on 1,869 (86.33%). The
disagreement is nearly one-directional: 283 people were high risk under Model A but low risk under Model
B, against only 13 the other way. Three mechanisms explain it. First, the cut-offs mean different things.
Model A flags predictions at or above the median of its own output, 4.592 on COMPAS’s 1–10 scale, so it
flags 50.07% of defendants by construction. Model B’s 0.5 is an absolute probability; because only 45.4%
of test defendants reoffended, it flags 37.60%. Second, the targets differ. Model A imitates COMPAS’s
proprietary score and adds 0.587 decile points for African-American defendants (p < 0.001), whereas
Model B, trained on real outcomes, finds a log-odds gap of only 0.098 (p = 0.158). Third, a linear model is
unbounded: Model A predicts from −1.48 to 15.67. The median rule also changes the error profile: Model
A’s flag has higher recall (67.7% versus 56.1%) but lower precision (61.3% versus 67.7%) and a
false-positive rate of 35.4% against 22.3%. Rankings still agree strongly (Spearman 0.960), so mismatches
cluster near the boundary: their mean Model B probability is 0.457 (range 0.358–0.566). Cutting Model B
at 0.449, which flags the same share as Model A, reduces mismatches to 220 (10.16%); so 76

I split by sex (A = male, n = 5,819; B = female, n = 1,395). No strict Simpson-style reversal exists: of 26

The robust finding is a stronger prior-record effect for women. Per standard deviation of priors_count the

Question 4
As a practitioner, how would you detect a hidden confound you cannot currently see? Propose one concrete,
real-world method.

As a practitioner I would run a covariate-augmentation and sensitivity audit rather than trust the
current feature set. Step 1: convene a domain-expert audit, with public defenders, probation officers and a
county data steward, to list factors that plausibly drive both exposure to the justice system and
reoffending but are absent from the file: employment, housing stability, substance-use treatment,
supervision conditions and neighbourhood policing intensity. Step 2: obtain proxies for a pilot sample, for
example census-tract arrest rates and unemployment joined by address, collected under data-protection
approval. Step 3: refit the model with each candidate and measure coefficient shifts in standard-error
units. My own experiment supplies the yardstick: omitting priors_count moved age by 5.9 SE and
race_African_American by 2.9 SE. I would pre-register a trigger, for example any key or protected-attribute
coefficient moving by more than 1 SE, which would send the model back for redesign. Step 4: for
confounders that cannot be measured, quantify robustness with an omitted-variable sensitivity analysis
(Cinelli and Hazlett, 2020) or an E-value (VanderWeele and Ding, 2017), asking how strong an
unmeasured variable would have to be, relative to priors_count, to erase the effect of interest. Step 5:
validate externally on another county or year and compare calibration within groups. Limitations are real:
experts only name confounders they can imagine, proxies are noisy, and the audit costs time and data
access. It is also unable to prove absence of confounding; it can only bound it. I would still prefer it to a
single hold-out accuracy figure, which in this dataset (67.9%) said nothing about the 4 coefficients that
changed significance when one variable disappeared.

Question 8
Should a policymaker prioritise demographic parity or equalized odds for this dataset? Cite the trade-off numbers
and acknowledge what is sacrificed.

I would recommend prioritising equalized odds, specifically parity in false-positive rates, over
demographic parity. The groups’ base rates differ (51.5% versus 41.1% in the test set), so demographic
parity would require equal flag rates regardless of what people did. At t = 0.45 the flag rates are 66.5%
(B) and 33.7% (A), and closing that gap by lowering the threshold to 0.25 (DP 0.507 → 0.805, +59%)
means flagging 93.9% of Group B and 75.6% of Group A: mass flagging, with overall accuracy falling to
55.6%. Equalized odds conditions on the actual outcome, so it targets the concrete harm in this setting:
non-reoffenders being wrongly detained or denied release, a direct loss of liberty whose burden can be
audited from outcomes alone. The false-positive ratio is 0.478 at t = 0.45 and 0.755 at t = 0.25. In this
model the two metrics rise together (r = 0.998; EO +58% over the same move), so choosing EO does not
sacrifice DP; what is sacrificed is predictive parity and accuracy. PP slips from 0.920 to 0.882 (4.2% lower)
and accuracy by 11.2 points. I find that acceptable because PP stays within 12% of parity, whereas a 0.48
false-positive ratio is not. Two cautions: EO, defined as a minimum of ratios, can hide a large gap in the
weaker ratio; and a race-blind model still gives DP 0.570 and EO 0.573 at t = 0.45, so the disparity runs
through proxies such as priors and age, and policy must also scrutinise those inputs. I would write this into
policy as a published ceiling on the false-positive ratio, audited annually.

10 Part 4 – Reflection essay
“If a policy-maker asked you: ‘just give me one number: should we deploy this model or not?’, what would you say,
and what is the limitation of that ‘one number’ answer that you discovered yourself through your analysis?”

Direct answer. I would recommend conditional deployment: advisory decision support only, at t =
0.45, with mandatory group-level monitoring, and not as an automated trigger for detention or
sentencing. The headline reason is that 66.8% overall accuracy conceals a false-positive rate of 49.0% for

Why a single number fails. First, overall accuracy hides subgroup gaps. The headline 67.9% at 0.5 says
nothing about who bears the errors: at t = 0.45 the false-positive ratio is 0.48 and the true-positive ratio
0.59, and even discrimination differs by group (test AUC 0.748 for African-American versus 0.689 for
Caucasian defendants); the pooled model also under-predicts Caucasian risk (0.385 predicted versus
0.411 observed). Second, the threshold is a values question, not a data-driven fact. Accuracy peaks at t =
0.55 (67.4%); the ratio metrics are “perfect” only at t = 0.05, where everyone is flagged and accuracy
(45.4%) falls below the 54.6% baseline. My own rule picks 0.45 for any accuracy tolerance from one to five
points and 0.35 from seven to ten, so the answer depends on a human choice about how much accuracy
to trade. Even the conventional 0.5 is a choice: it misses 43.9% of reoffenders. Third, fairness metrics
conflict. Base rates differ (51.5% versus 41.1%), so predictive parity (0.98 at t = 0.55) and equalized odds
(0.34 at the same threshold) cannot both hold; improving EO to 0.76 at t = 0.25 flags 778 non-reoffenders
instead of 143. Fourth, the model fails confidently on predictable subsets: 90 test cases (4.2%) are
confident errors, concentrated among defendants with five or more priors (false positives) and older
defendants with almost none (false negatives), and a single accuracy number gives no hint of where. Fifth,
hidden confounds remain unknowable: omitting one variable moved the race coefficient from 0.098 to
0.302 and made 4 coefficients significant, so any variable I have not measured could do the same. Finally,
every ratio carries sampling error: with 747 Caucasian test defendants, DP at t = 0.45 has a 95%
bootstrap interval of [0.456, 0.562] and PP [0.819, 1.036], which cannot distinguish parity from a gap of
roughly 18%. A policy-maker given one number would inherit all of these problems without being able to
see them.
Grounding in evidence. Part 1: 296 mismatched flags (13.67%), median 4.592 versus the 0.5 threshold.
Part 2.1: priors effect 1.024 (women) versus 0.666 (men), p = 0.0016. Part 2.2: race coefficient 0.098 →
0.302. Part 2.3: 90 confident errors against 88.4 expected (21 false positives and 29 false negatives in the
subset). Part 2.4 at t = 0.45: DP 0.507, EO 0.478, PP 0.920, accuracy 66.8%.
Closing. The meta-lesson is that responsible deployment means publishing the trade-offs, not hiding
them inside one metric. Decision-makers should see the threshold, the error rates by group, the confident
failures and the unresolved confounds, and be able to challenge each. A model card carrying these tables,
rather than a score alone, is what I would hand to the policy-maker.
(499 words)

11 Limitations and conclusion
Limitations. (1) The target is two-year re-arrest recorded in one Florida county, which measures policing
as well as behaviour; label bias cannot be tested with this file. (2) The analysis uses one random split
(seed 450); the fairness estimates for Group A rest on 747 test defendants, so bootstrap intervals are wide
(for example DP at t = 0.45: [0.456, 0.562]) and values at t ≥ 0.85 rest on very few flagged people. (3)
Section 2.1 was a search, so I applied a Bonferroni correction across 26 comparisons and selected the
reported split after the scan; the selection is declared, not hidden. (4) Race was included as a predictor to
expose its effect; removing it did not remove the disparity (Appendix B). (5) Logistic coefficients are
non-collapsible and priors may be a mediator, so no causal claim is made. (6) The recommended threshold
depends on a stated 2-point accuracy tolerance. (7) The EO definition (a minimum of ratios) can mask the
weaker disparity; Table 13 reports both ratios. Conclusion. A simple, transparent logistic model predicts
two-year recidivism with accuracy 67.9% and AUC 0.734, yet every stress test exposed something the
headline metrics hide: disagreement with the linear score (13.67%), coefficient fragility, structured
confident errors and unequal error rates across groups. The defensible conclusion is conditional, advisory
use with explicit, published trade-offs.
